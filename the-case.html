The argument

The case for repeal

Three pillars. Each one stated plainly, with the strongest version of the other side's answer — and our response to it. Footnoted to the Act and to Hansard throughout.

1 · Equality 2 · Democratic mandate 3 · Sovereignty of Parliament
01

Equality before the law

The first promise of a democracy is that the law sees citizens, not categories. The Statewide Treaty Act breaks that promise — not rhetorically, but structurally. It creates a second electoral roll, a permanent representative body for one group, and statutory access to ministers that no other Victorian community holds. Ten of those differences are laid out, clause by clause, in Two Victorias.

This is not an argument about whether disadvantage exists. It does, and it demands serious policy. It is an argument about how a state may respond: with programs, funding and services — which any government can deliver under equal laws — or with parallel civic institutions defined by ancestry, which no government should.

Their strongest answer

"Equal treatment of unequal situations entrenches inequality. The treaty doesn't take rights from anyone — it adds accountability for outcomes, through a body elected by First Peoples, after a decade-long public process."

Our response

Every additional statutory right in the Act is held by citizens of one ancestry and not another — that is the definition of unequal civic status, however benign the intent. Accountability for outcomes does not require a permanent, parallel representative structure; it requires a government willing to be measured. Programs can close gaps. Institutions defined by ancestry entrench them.

02

No democratic mandate

In October 2023, Victorians voted No to a constitutional Voice — including majorities in most Victorian electorates. Eighteen months later, Parliament legislated a more far-reaching body, with statutory powers the proposed Voice never had, without putting the question to Victorians at any election. The Bill was pushed through the Assembly in a 13-hour overnight sitting on 16 October 2025.

A change to who is represented, and how, in the machinery of the state is not ordinary legislation. It is a change to the civic compact — and it was made over the heads of the people it governs.

Their strongest answer

"Parliaments legislate; that is representative democracy. Treaty was Labor policy across multiple elections that Labor won, the referendum was a federal question, and the Assembly process ran for years in public."

Our response

A line in a platform is not a mandate for a permanent constitutional-scale change that the same electorate had just rejected in substance. If the government believes Victorians support this Act, the test is simple: November 2026. We intend to make that election the vote Victorians were never given.

03

The sovereignty of Parliament

Parliament must answer to all the people, equally — that is what makes its sovereignty legitimate. The Act compromises this in both directions: it obliges Parliament to test every future Bill against the treaty, and it grants one statutory body rights to question ministers and compel meetings that sit outside the parliamentary chamber and outside the reach of voters.

Defenders say no veto exists, and that is true. But constitutions are shaped by process as much as by power. A standing treaty test on all legislation, administered with reference to a body only some citizens elect, bends the ordinary lawmaking of the state around an institution most Victorians have no vote in.

Their strongest answer

"Parliament remains sovereign — it can amend or repeal the Act at any time, compatibility statements are advisory, and the Charter of Human Rights has required similar statements since 2006 without the sky falling."

Our response

We agree Parliament can repeal it — that is exactly what we ask voters to authorise. The Charter comparison undercuts itself: the Charter protects rights held by every Victorian equally. A treaty test is owed to a treaty signed with one part of the community, policed by bodies one part of the community elects. The precedent grows; it does not sit still.
